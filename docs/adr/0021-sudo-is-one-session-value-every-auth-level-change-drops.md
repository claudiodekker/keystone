# Sudo is one session value that every auth-level change drops

A change to how an account signs in should rest on a proof made moments ago, not on a session that may be twelve hours old or restored by a cookie. Sudo is that proof's lifetime: a short grant on the signed-in session. The grant is security state like the sign-in itself, so the same questions apply to it. Where does it live, who may write it, and what makes sure a stale one never survives?

- **It lives in the session phase, which the guard drives.** The session holds one phase record: a pending sign-in, a registration, a sudo-in-progress, or a grant with the time it was granted and its subnet. It sits next to the epoch and the sign-in time (ADR 0005). Whatever a later step holds takes the place of the one before rather than sitting beside it, so a session can't hold a pending sign-in, a registration and sudo at once, or two answers to "does it have sudo". A malformed record counts as nothing held.
- **A grant can't slide.** Reading a grant never stamps it again: it ends `sudo.lifetime_seconds` (15 minutes) after it was granted, however busy the session is. The lifetime is applied when the grant is read, so lowering the setting shortens grants already held. A grant whose time is in the future is forgotten, as a session's sign-in time is (ADR 0013).
- **Every change of auth level drops it.** Holding a sign-in, passing a second factor, signing in, restoring from a remember-me cookie and ending sudo each rotate the session id, close the ceremony slots and forget the session phase. Sign-out, expiry and demotion invalidate the whole session. A flow added later drops the grant by changing the auth level the way every other does, with nothing to remember. This matters for a session that outlived its absolute lifetime and is restored by its cookie (ADR 0020): the session's data is kept, and without the drop a grant a minute old would be carried into a session nobody proved anything for.
- **The mover's session keeps it.** A user's own change that ends their other sessions re-stamps and rotates their session, which is not an auth-level change (ADR 0014). They just proved who they are to make that change, so their grant stays.
- **Only a real sign-in brings it.** Every flow that completes a sign-in brings sudo without asking for it: the first factor, the challenge and the enrollment. A remembered return never brings a grant, and drops any grant the session held. The guard records `sudo.granted` with the reason `keystone.sign_in` itself, so no flow can bring a grant without the row.
- **A grant is bound to a subnet, and none is made without one.** The subnet is the /24 of an IPv4 address, IPv4-mapped IPv6 unwrapped first, else the /64. A sign-in from an address that can't be parsed as an IP address completes with no grant and no `sudo.granted`, rather than failing the sign-in or holding a grant that any network could use. Binding to the whole address would end the grant every time a phone or an IPv6 privacy address changed. Binding to nothing would let a copied session cookie carry sudo to any network.
- **Ending sudo is an auth-level change, and records its own event.** Ending it records `sudo.revoked` only when a live grant was ended, so repeating `DELETE sudo.end` adds nothing to the trail. A grant used from another subnet is ended with `sudo.network_changed` and never `sudo.revoked`. Ending a grant always records its row.

## Consequences

- A grant lives and dies with its session. There is no table, no index of grants and no way to list or revoke another session's grant, except by ending that session.
- An account whose requests reach the app from an address that isn't an IP address, such as behind a proxy that isn't trusted and passes a hostname, never has sudo after signing in.
- Behind a load balancer whose address the app doesn't resolve to the client's, every grant is bound to the balancer's subnet, and the binding stops telling networks apart.
- `sudo.granted` adds one row to the account's trail for every sign-in.
- Lowering `sudo.lifetime_seconds` takes effect for grants already held. Raising it revives a grant that had run out but whose session nothing has read since.
